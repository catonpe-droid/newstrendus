How much rent can I afford on $80,000 a year?

Answer: On an $80,000 annual gross salary, a 30% housing budget is $2,000 a month for rent plus utilities. If you pay $200 in utilities separately, that leaves $1,800 for rent before any additional debt adjustment. With $300 in monthly debt entered in this calculator, its more conservative illustrative result is $1,500. These figures are planning examples, not landlord qualification limits; compare them with take-home pay and actual recurring costs.

How much should I budget for rent each month?

Quick answer: Start with a housing amount you can reliably cover from take-home pay after debt, groceries, transportation, insurance, childcare and savings. As one gross-income planning exercise, 25% to 30% of annual pretax income divided by 12 gives a monthly rent-plus-utilities range. This is not a minimum, guaranteed safe budget or rental qualification standard. Housing burden statistics commonly use the 30% rent-plus-utilities benchmark, as explained in the U.S. Government Accountability Office.

How much rent can I afford on my salary?

Quick estimate: multiply your annual pretax income by 0.30, then divide by 12 for a monthly rent-plus-utilities starting point. For example, $50,000 a year gives $1,250 per month for rent and utilities; with $150 in separately paid utilities, that leaves about $1,100 for rent before considering other bills. Try a lower percentage if debt, childcare, transportation or savings needs make that amount uncomfortable.

Important distinction: the 30% housing-cost benchmark includes utilities; it is not a landlord approval rule. The U.S. Government Accountability Office explains rent burden as rent plus utilities relative to income. This calculator additionally subtracts the debt payment you enter as a conservative personal-budget adjustment, rather than treating debt as part of that official housing-cost ratio.

How to think about rent affordability

A common starting exercise is to allocate a share of gross income to housing. For example, at $60,000 annual gross income, a 30% housing allocation equals $1,500 per month before subtracting the debt and utility amounts entered in this tool. The calculator is intentionally conservative when it deducts those additional costs; actual landlord screening may use a different method. This calculator uses a customizable percentage and then deducts the monthly debts and utilities you enter. It is a simple planning method, not an approved underwriting formula for every apartment.

What the calculator's result means

Quick answer: The displayed amount is a scenario-based estimate of monthly rent, not a landlord's income qualification or a recommended maximum. The tool first takes your chosen percentage of annual gross income divided by 12, then subtracts the monthly debt payments and utilities you entered. For example, $60,000 in annual gross income at 30% produces a $1,500 starting housing allocation; entering $300 in monthly debt payments and $180 in utilities produces an illustrative $1,020 rent estimate. The deduction for debt is an extra conservative planning choice, not part of the conventional 30% housing benchmark. Do not subtract expenses twice if your chosen housing percentage already accounts for them.

Compare the estimate with your take-home pay, actual utility bills, renter's insurance, transportation and emergency savings. In some apartments utilities are included in rent; enter zero for any included cost to avoid double counting. A landlord may instead use a gross-income multiple, credit review or other screening criteria.

Why costs vary by household and city

Your effective budget changes with commuting costs, medical expenses, childcare, savings goals, utility arrangements and local housing conditions. A landlord may apply a different income-screening rule and require up-front payments such as a security deposit or broker fees.

Budget before looking at listings

Test several percentages rather than treating any single ratio as universally safe. Leave room for moving costs and emergencies, and remember that rent increases and utility bills can change. The calculator uses gross income; your net-pay cash flow may require a more conservative target.
