Mississippi Senate Race 2026: Why Colom–Hyde-Smith Is Uncalled and the November 10 Deadline Matters

An unusually narrow Mississippi Senate race is still in the counting stage. Here is the dated result, the precise voter deadlines and why a media projection is not the same as certification.

By NewsTrendus Editorial Desk · November 5, 2026 · Primary-source and local-report review · Representative photograph

This is a source-linked desk explainer, not first-hand reporting. Research and drafting may use AI-assisted tools. Follow cited records and our editorial and corrections policy.

At a glance, November 5: Mississippi’s U.S. Senate race between Democrat Scott Colom and Republican incumbent Cindy Hyde-Smith remains uncalled. An Associated Press election display updated early November 5 showed Colom with 464,926 votes and Hyde-Smith with 463,788, a 1,138-vote difference; independent Ty Pinkins had 19,934. These are unofficial, time-stamped figures, not a certified winner.

A close election is not finished simply because most precincts have reported. Mississippi voters and national readers are searching for a definitive result after the November 3, 2026 midterm vote. The unusually small Colom–Hyde-Smith margin has shifted as counties upload tallies, and the state is still processing legally eligible ballots. The Associated Press results display provides a changing snapshot; Mississippi Today’s local account explains the outstanding ballots and how both campaigns are observing the process.

What is the current Mississippi Senate count?

The AP data available early Thursday listed Colom at 49.0%, Hyde-Smith at 48.9% and Pinkins at about 2.1%, with about 96% of the estimated vote counted. Because this tally is changing, do not reuse the 1,138-vote figure without its date. The AP and other outlets reported much smaller margins earlier Wednesday; those observations are not contradictions once their timestamps are considered.

Votes reported by a county are not automatically final statewide results. A figure for estimated votes counted is also different from a legal declaration that every eligible ballot has been processed. The distinction matters when a race is close enough that even a limited batch of additional valid ballots could change who is ahead.

Why November 10 is the next important date

Mississippi’s Secretary of State election reminders say absentee ballots sent by mail must be postmarked by Election Day, November 3, and received by county circuit clerks by November 10. Voters who cast an affidavit ballot because they did not have acceptable photo ID also have until November 10 to present identification to their circuit clerk. These are separate processes, and a ballot is counted only if the applicable eligibility rules are met.

State officials reported around 3,600 outstanding absentee-by-mail ballots on Wednesday, with the number of affidavit ballots not yet settled. That does not mean 3,600 additional votes for either candidate: some envelopes may never arrive on time, some ballots may be rejected under applicable rules, and the voters’ choices cannot be inferred from their ballot type. The state’s November 2 absentee report gives earlier statewide requested, sent and returned counts, not a final election-night balance.

When do counties certify, and when is the official result declared?

Mississippi reporting says counties have until November 13 to certify and submit results, with a statewide declaration expected later in the process. The Wall Street Journal’s November 5 account identifies December 3 as the state declaration date. Check the election office for amendments to that timeline or court proceedings that could change it. An unofficial newsroom tally on November 5 should not be described as the final certified election outcome.

Does Mississippi have an automatic recount or a runoff?

ABC News, citing Mississippi’s Secretary of State, reports there is no automatic recount triggered by a close margin and no ordinary candidate-request procedure to order one. A legally grounded court challenge can lead to further ballot review. It is inaccurate to say the current margin automatically activates a recount. Mississippi’s U.S. Senate contest is decided by the greatest number of votes, without a second-round runoff merely because the leading candidate is below 50%.

The result is politically significant, but its significance is not a license to project a winner prematurely. Both campaign statements and social-media claims should be checked against official county records and date-stamped independent counts.

How readers can verify the next update

  1. Check the Mississippi Secretary of State elections portal and county circuit clerk for the governing deadlines.
  2. Compare that official status with a date-stamped AP results page, noting the counted-vote estimate and the actual timestamp.
  3. Separate an election-night projection from county certification and any court order. A lead is not the same as a final legal result.
