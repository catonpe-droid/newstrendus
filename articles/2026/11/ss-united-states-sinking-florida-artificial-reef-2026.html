SS United States Sinking: Florida Reef Location and Facts

A record-setting American ocean liner began a controlled sinking off Florida on November 11, 2026. Here is what is confirmed about its new reef location and what remains to be verified.

Quick answer: The 990-foot SS United States began a deliberate sinking on November 11, 2026, near Destin, Florida, as part of a project to create a major artificial reef. Reuters and Associated Press confirmed the controlled flooding process. Their reporting does not establish that the ship had already settled into its final seabed position.

Where is the SS United States sinking?

The designated site lies about 22 nautical miles southwest of Destin–Fort Walton Beach in the Gulf of Mexico, according to the SS United States Conservancy. The planned resting depth is approximately 180 feet, with the upper deck several dozen feet below the surface. The ship's exact final position and readiness for public diving require post-deployment confirmation. The site is not an invitation to approach ongoing marine operations.

Why did the sinking happen on November 11 instead of November 12?

Earlier announcements scheduled the operation for Thursday, November 12, weather permitting. Officials moved it to Wednesday, November 11, over weather considerations, AP reported. The process began Wednesday afternoon. A 6abc live report said the ship had been fully flooded and deployment was underway by evening. That is a progress update, not independent confirmation of the final resting position.

How is the ship being turned into an artificial reef?

Crews prepared the former passenger liner for controlled flooding rather than using explosives. They removed fuel and environmentally unsuitable materials, opened passages and cut large holes in decks to manage the descent. More than 400 portholes were removed, according to AP. The aim is to provide a durable structure where marine life can eventually settle. Whether the reef delivers its anticipated ecological benefits can only be assessed over time; the environmental outcome is not established by the sinking alone.

At 990 feet, the SS United States is longer than the Titanic and the former aircraft carrier USS Oriskany, deployed as a reef off Pensacola in 2006. Organizers describe the new project as the world's largest artificial reef based on the vessel's size. The Gulf Coast already contains hundreds of artificial reef sites, but access and safe diving conditions vary.

What made the SS United States historically important?

Built in Virginia, the ocean liner set a transatlantic speed record on its first voyage in 1952 and crossed the Atlantic hundreds of times. It carried prominent passengers and became a symbol of American maritime engineering. Retired in 1969 as jet travel grew, it spent decades inactive, including many years moored in Philadelphia. Various proposals for commercial reuse never materialized. The reef project is a new chapter for a vessel that otherwise faced the prospect of being scrapped.

What will be saved for the museum?

The Conservancy says it has secured important artifacts including the ship's iconic funnels, radar mast and anchor for a planned land-based museum in the Destin–Fort Walton Beach area. The museum remains a future project, not an already-open attraction. Preservation advocates hope it will tell the ship's engineering and social history while the hull becomes an underwater destination.

Can tourists dive to the SS United States now?

Do not assume so. Diving access depends on completion of the operation, official inspections, local regulations, sea conditions and operator safety guidance. The project is intended to support marine habitat and tourism, but neither immediate access nor projected visitor numbers should be presented as confirmed. Interested divers should check official county and Conservancy updates before planning a trip.

What happens next?

The key next milestones are confirmation of the ship's final settled position, any safety assessment and the eventual opening of permitted diving visits. The estimated project cost has been reported at approximately $13 million to $13.5 million depending on the accounting scope. No claim of guaranteed tourism revenue is supported.
