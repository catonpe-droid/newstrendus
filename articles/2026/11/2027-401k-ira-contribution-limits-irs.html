2027 401(k) Limit Rises to $25,500: IRA and Catch-Ups

The IRS has set new workplace retirement contribution limits for 2027. Here is how the higher 401(k) ceiling compares with IRA limits, catch-up contributions and income restrictions.

Quick answer: The IRS announced November 12, 2026 that the standard employee 401(k) contribution ceiling will rise from $24,500 in 2026 to $25,500 in 2027. The IRA annual limit remains $7,500. Different catch-up limits apply depending on age and plan type.

2027 retirement contribution limits at a glance

Contribution category20262027
Standard 401(k), 403(b), most governmental 457 and TSP employee deferral$24,500$25,500
Standard IRA contribution$7,500$7,500
401(k) catch-up, generally age 50+$8,000$8,500
401(k) special catch-up, ages 60–63$11,250$11,750
IRA catch-up, age 50+$1,100$1,100

Figures reflect the IRS's 2027 announcement. They describe statutory ceilings, not guaranteed amounts that every worker can contribute. Plan rules, compensation and other limitations may apply.

How much can workers age 50 or older contribute?

For most eligible workplace plans, workers age 50 or older may contribute an additional $8,500 in 2027, making a combined employee deferral ceiling of $34,000. Workers who are ages 60, 61, 62 or 63 may qualify for a larger $11,750 catch-up instead, bringing their combined ceiling to $37,250. The higher catch-up replaces, rather than adds to, the ordinary catch-up amount.

Eligibility depends on the type of plan and its rules. Employees should confirm their specific arrangements with their benefits administrator. The IRS explains how elective deferral limits and employer contributions differ; the headline $25,500 is not a combined employee-and-employer contribution maximum.

What changes for traditional and Roth IRAs?

The combined annual contribution limit for traditional and Roth IRAs stays at $7,500 in 2027, with an additional $1,100 catch-up for eligible people age 50 and older. That generally means a potential $8,600 annual IRA contribution for eligible older savers, subject to compensation and other rules. Contributing to both types of IRA does not double the shared limit.

For 2027, the Roth IRA income phase-out range increases to $158,000–$173,000 for single filers and heads of household, and $250,000–$260,000 for married couples filing jointly. A phase-out means the allowable direct Roth contribution can shrink as income rises. Traditional IRA deductions have separate rules that also depend on workplace retirement coverage.

What should U.S. savers do before 2027?

First, check whether your workplace plan permits the relevant catch-up contributions and whether payroll elections need to be updated for January. Second, distinguish the employee salary-deferral limit from total plan funding, including employer contributions. Third, review your expected filing status and income before assuming you can make a deductible traditional IRA or direct Roth IRA contribution.

For context on other 2027 tax-year adjustments, see NewsTrendus' 2027 federal tax brackets and standard deduction guide. The IRS's IRA overview explains the basic difference between traditional and Roth accounts. These are different decisions: a higher contribution ceiling does not by itself establish that an account is suitable for an individual.

What is confirmed, and what happens next?

The numbers above come from the IRS's November 12 release and its accompanying Notice 2026-66. They are limits for tax year 2027, not a change to 2026 contributions. Individual payroll elections, employer plan restrictions and tax circumstances still determine what a saver may actually contribute. Check the official IRS guidance and your plan administrator before making changes.
