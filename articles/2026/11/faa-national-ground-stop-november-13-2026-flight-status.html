FAA Nationwide Ground Stop: Flight Status and Traveler Steps

A rare national departure halt followed FAA communications problems. Check live flight status and understand what airlines owe travelers.

By NewsTrendus Editorial Desk · November 13, 2026 · Source-linked analysis

This is a source-linked desk explainer, not first-hand reporting. Research and drafting may use AI-assisted tools. Follow the cited source links and read our editorial and corrections policy.

Representative licensed photograph of an airplane on a runway; not a specific affected flight or the November 13 incident. Photo: Drinu Cutajar / Pexels.

Quick answer: The Federal Aviation Administration ordered a nationwide stop on departing flights Friday, November 13, 2026, after communications problems affected air traffic control centers. Aircraft already flying could land; departures on the ground were held. As of the reports reviewed, officials had not provided a verified nationwide restart time. Travelers should check their airline's live flight status and FAA National Airspace System Status rather than assume an old alert still applies.

Why were U.S. flights grounded November 13?

According to Associated Press reporting, communications issues affected regional air traffic control centers that direct aircraft between airports. The FAA imposed a national ground stop for safety. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said FAA technicians were investigating the communications failure. The initial interruption had also affected departures in Chicago, Reuters reported.

Officials had not publicly confirmed a technical root cause in the initial reports. Homeland Security officials said there was no evidence at that point of a malicious cyberattack; that preliminary assessment is not a final forensic finding. Claims that the outage was caused by a particular software update or another unrelated service outage should not be treated as established facts.

Are flights still grounded? How to check the current status

This article describes the November 13 ground stop, not a live FAA status feed. An emergency departure restriction can be lifted, narrowed or replaced by airport-specific delays after an article is published. For a trip today, verify the most recent information in this order:

  1. Open your airline's app or official website and look up your specific flight number and departure date.
  2. Review the FAA's official NAS Status page for ground stops, ground-delay programs and airport advisories. A national alert may differ from an individual airport's status.
  3. Check the departure airport's official notices and airline messages before leaving home.
  4. If a flight is canceled, use the airline's rebooking or refund channel and keep screenshots of notices and receipts.

A flight shown as scheduled can still change. The FAA status page describes air-traffic restrictions, while the airline controls its own passenger itinerary and rebooking decisions.

What happens to refunds and rebooking after an FAA ground stop?

The U.S. Department of Transportation's refund guidance says passengers generally qualify for a refund when an airline cancels a flight or makes a significant schedule change and the passenger chooses not to travel or accept an alternative. Whether meals, hotels or other expenses are covered depends on the circumstances and the carrier's commitments; an air-traffic-control outage is not automatically an airline-controlled disruption.

For any replacement ticket, compare the airline's proposed itinerary with your actual arrival needs. Ask the carrier to explain the applicable policy before paying for an expensive alternative. If you booked through a travel agency, check who must process the change or refund.

What is confirmed, and what remains uncertain?

The confirmed event is a nationwide FAA departure stop on November 13 tied to communications problems. The AP account carried by WBUR confirms the FAA safety notice and the investigation. Reuters says flights already in the air were allowed to land and international departures were considered case by case. Early estimates of delays or cancellations changed rapidly; this article deliberately does not present a preliminary tally as a final total.

As of the source review for this article, a definitive restoration time, final incident report and precise root cause had not been independently verified. Follow later FAA advisories for those answers. This is not the same issue as the separate Microsoft 365 disruption, and no connection has been established.

What comes next for U.S. aviation systems?

The incident renews attention on the reliability of air traffic control communications. Reuters notes that Congress approved $12.5 billion in 2025 for modernization and that the FAA and White House sought additional funding on November 13. These funding figures describe modernization plans, not proof of what caused this outage. The operational investigation and official FAA notices are the relevant sources for any confirmed cause.
