Dollar General FTC Lawsuit: 30 States and Checkout Prices

Ten more states joined the FTC's Dollar General pricing lawsuit, bringing the total to 30. See the states, allegations and shoppers' next steps.

By NewsTrendus Editorial Desk · November 22, 2026 · Source-linked consumer news · Representative photograph

This is source-linked desk reporting, not firsthand reporting or a legal opinion. The FTC allegations remain disputed and are not court findings. AI-assisted drafting may be used; see our editorial and corrections policy.

Representative licensed grocery-shopping photograph, not a Dollar General store or a photograph of any incident in the lawsuit. Photo: Gustavo Fring / Pexels.

Quick answer: On November 20, the Federal Trade Commission said ten more states joined its lawsuit against Dollar General over alleged gaps between displayed shelf prices and prices charged at checkout. The lawsuit now includes 30 states. These are allegations before a court, not a determination of liability; the FTC announcement does not provide a consumer refund application.

What changed in the Dollar General lawsuit on November 20?

The Federal Trade Commission announced an amended complaint on Friday, November 20, 2026. Ten additional state governments became plaintiffs alongside the FTC and the 20 states that were part of the original October case. Reuters independently reported the expansion the same day.

The government alleges that customers repeatedly faced higher register prices than the amounts displayed on shelves, tags or advertisements, and that the company did not adequately correct the problem. Dollar General disputes the accusation that it willfully disregarded customers. A court, rather than the FTC's announcement alone, will decide the contested claims.

Which ten states joined the Dollar General FTC case?

According to the FTC's amended complaint announcement, the new states are Delaware, Georgia, Kentucky, Maine, Minnesota, New Mexico, Oregon, Rhode Island, South Carolina and South Dakota. They join the original 20: Alabama, Arizona, Arkansas, California, Connecticut, Florida, Illinois, Iowa, Kansas, Louisiana, Michigan, Mississippi, Nebraska, New Jersey, North Carolina, Ohio, Oklahoma, Tennessee, Utah and Wisconsin.

Being part of the litigation does not mean every Dollar General transaction in a state was incorrect, and the complaint does not establish that every shopper is entitled to compensation. The case was filed in federal court in the Middle District of Tennessee. Consumers should avoid interpreting a state joining the case as the opening of a refund program.

What is alleged about shelf prices and checkout totals?

In its October 26 original lawsuit announcement, the FTC alleged systemic discrepancies between advertised prices and what shoppers paid at the register. The amended complaint continues that dispute while expanding the participating states.

A shelf-tag mismatch can be difficult to detect when a basket contains many low-cost household products. The practical issue is not simply whether a receipt shows a total, but whether individual items match the advertised price at the time of purchase. Promotions, taxes, quantities and coupon conditions can also alter a legitimate checkout total, so a perceived difference should be checked item by item.

What has Dollar General said in response?

In an October 26 company statement, Dollar General said its goal was complete consistency between shelf and register prices and acknowledged that occasional discrepancies can arise. The company said store teams are instructed to correct errors and that it had improved its pricing processes. It rejected the suggestion that it had intentionally disregarded its customers and said it intended to defend the case.

The November 20 filing adds states; it does not itself resolve the disagreement between regulators and the retailer.

Can Dollar General shoppers claim a refund now?

The November 20 FTC announcement does not establish an approved claim form, a payment deadline or an automatic refund amount for this lawsuit. A lawsuit is not the same as a final settlement. Do not submit personal or banking details to websites that promise an FTC Dollar General payout without verifying an official notice.

Shoppers who notice a difference can review their receipts against shelf tags or advertised offers, ask the store to check the item, and save any relevant receipt or advertising evidence. The appropriate reporting channel for a consumer issue may depend on the state and the nature of the complaint. The FTC provides the official ReportFraud.ftc.gov portal for deceptive practices, and state attorneys general publish consumer-complaint guidance.

Practical checkout-price checklist

  1. Check the exact item: Compare size, variant, bundle quantity and promotion dates before assuming a shelf sign applies.
  2. Keep documentation: Retain the receipt and, where permitted, a photograph of the shelf price or advertised offer.
  3. Ask for a correction: Point out the difference to the cashier or store manager; policies and state consumer laws vary.
  4. Use official channels: Report unresolved deceptive pricing concerns through appropriate state consumer protection agencies or the FTC's public complaint portal.
  5. Avoid fake refund links: Monitor the FTC's Dollar General case record for actual orders or settlement developments.

Is this the same as the Family Dollar settlement?

No. The Family Dollar pricing settlement covered separately by NewsTrendus concerns a different retailer and legal matter. Do not use a Family Dollar refund or settlement page as evidence that a Dollar General payout is available. The November 20 Dollar General development is specifically an expansion of plaintiffs in an unresolved lawsuit.

What happens next?

The court process will determine how the allegations are litigated and whether there is any judgment, negotiated resolution or consumer redress. The addition of states is a meaningful procedural development, not a finding that the FTC has won its case. We will distinguish later verified court actions from allegations and update this article when a substantive development can be documented.
