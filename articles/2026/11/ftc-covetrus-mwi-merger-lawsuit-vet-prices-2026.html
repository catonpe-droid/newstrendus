FTC Covetrus-MWI Lawsuit: What Pet Owners Should Know

Federal regulators are challenging a $3.5 billion veterinary-distribution deal. The lawsuit raises questions about clinic supply costs, but it does not establish that pet-care bills have increased.

By NewsTrendus Editorial Desk · November 17, 2026 · Source-linked desk reporting · Representative photograph

Independent source-linked analysis of a pending antitrust complaint, not firsthand court reporting or legal advice. Allegations are not judicial findings. See our editorial and corrections policy.

Representative licensed photograph of paperwork and a phone; not an image from the reported lawsuit. Photographer: Leeloo The First / Pexels.

Quick answer: On November 13, 2026, the Federal Trade Commission and state attorneys general filed a federal lawsuit seeking to block Covetrus's proposed $3.5 billion acquisition of MWI Veterinary Supply from Cencora. The case is pending. The FTC argues the combination could reduce competition and ultimately raise costs for veterinary clinics and pet owners; Covetrus disputes that assessment.

What is the FTC's Covetrus-MWI lawsuit about?

The FTC's November 13 announcement says Covetrus and MWI compete to distribute medicines, vaccines, surgical supplies, gloves and other products to U.S. veterinary clinics. The agency alleges that combining two major national distributors would eliminate meaningful head-to-head price competition. Its official case docket identifies the matter as pending in the Southern District of New York, not as a completed ban or final court ruling.

The FTC says the two distributors have competed on rebates and service terms, including in response to online price-comparison tools. These details matter to veterinary practices because supply purchasing is a recurring operating expense, but the lawsuit itself does not quantify any specific future increase in an individual pet owner's bill.

Will the merger make veterinary visits more expensive?

That is a concern raised by the government, not an established outcome. The FTC argues reduced distributor competition could increase wholesale costs and weaken service quality, potentially affecting prices paid by pet owners. A clinic's final charges also depend on labor, facilities, treatment complexity and its own pricing decisions. There is no verified nationwide veterinary fee increase attributable to this proposed deal.

Reuters independently reported that Covetrus challenged the government's market definition. The company argues that veterinary practices have alternative suppliers and purchasing channels. Readers should distinguish this company response from the FTC's allegations rather than treating either position as a court finding.

Who is involved and where was the case filed?

Covetrus, its private-equity owners Clayton, Dubilier & Rice and TPG, Cencora and MWI are named in the FTC case. The federal complaint was filed in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York. The FTC announcement says attorneys general from 13 states joined the challenge. The agency voted 2-0 to authorize staff to seek an injunction.

The transaction was announced in February 2026. As Reuters reported at the time, the proposed sale was part of Cencora's effort to concentrate on pharmaceutical distribution. The new litigation concerns whether the planned consolidation is legally permissible, not a recall of veterinary medicines or a warning that clinics should stop using products from either company.

What should pet owners and veterinary clinics do now?

Pet owners do not need to file a claim, change veterinarians or expect a refund because of this lawsuit. If concerned about care costs, ask a clinic for an itemized estimate and discuss clinically appropriate options with the veterinarian. Clinics assessing supply contracts should monitor their own vendor terms and the court case rather than assuming an immediate change in distributor availability.

For reliable updates, consult the FTC's case record. It currently labels the matter pending. A future injunction, settlement, trial ruling or deal abandonment would be a separate development requiring fresh verification. Readers interested in other consumer-protection actions can see our Family Dollar settlement status guide and FTC auto-pricing case explainer; neither is part of the veterinary merger case.

What happens next?

The parties must address the challenge through the federal court process. A complaint seeks judicial relief; it does not by itself establish liability or mean a judge has blocked the acquisition. The key next verified milestone is a court order or official case update. This report reflects information available November 17, 2026, without predicting a hearing date or outcome.
