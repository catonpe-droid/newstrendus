FDA Starts Calling AI 'Super Intelligence': What Changes

Starting November 16, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration says it will use “Super Intelligence” (SI) instead of “Artificial Intelligence” (AI) in new public-facing materials where legally permitted. The agency explicitly says the change does not impose new submission or labeling requirements on drug and medical-device companies.

Quick answer: The FDA's terminology change takes effect for newly issued public communications on November 16, 2026. Existing regulations remain in effect, and companies do not have to rename products, change labeling or revise FDA submissions simply because of this executive-branch terminology policy.

What changed at FDA on November 16?

In its implementation notice, FDA says it will use the terms “Super Intelligence” and “SI” in newly issued public-facing documents and communications, where applicable and legally permitted. It is implementing Executive Order 14434, signed September 29 and published in the Federal Register October 2.

The order concerns federal agency terminology. It uses the existing statutory definition of AI for the systems covered by the new term; the announcement is not evidence that every covered system has become more capable or meets the research meaning of artificial superintelligence.

Do drugmakers or device manufacturers need to change filings?

No new FDA filing requirement arises from the renaming itself. FDA specifically states that the executive order creates no new requirement for sponsors, applicants, manufacturers or other parties submitting information to the agency. The agency includes established names, proprietary names, trade names and labeling in that clarification.

For a company preparing a regulatory submission, the practical distinction is between terminology the agency uses in its own communications and requirements that already apply to a product. Sponsors should continue to follow the applicable FDA statutes, regulations, guidance and submission instructions, rather than treating the new term as a separate approval pathway.

Will older FDA guidance and regulations be rewritten?

Not immediately. FDA says previously issued guidance documents, regulations and Federal Register notices remain in effect as written. It plans to adjust terminology in existing public-facing documents when those documents next receive substantive revisions, while ordinary web-content and form updates will follow normal maintenance schedules.

Where a statute or regulation itself uses “artificial intelligence” or “AI,” FDA says it will continue quoting and citing that legal wording accurately. That exception matters for researchers and compliance teams searching older documents: the two terms may appear alongside each other during the transition.

How does the FDA policy compare with other federal agencies?

The National Institutes of Health also describes an agency transition to “SI” under the same order. The White House's September 29 fact sheet outlines the administration-wide rationale. Separately, Associated Press reporting described early State Department instructions to use the new wording, underscoring that the shift extends beyond health regulation.

The distinction between a government vocabulary decision and technical capability has also been discussed in independent legal analysis. None of these sources establishes that the FDA has changed medical-device approval standards on November 16.

What should U.S. readers and regulated businesses do next?

For readers of FDA notices, search for both “AI” and “SI” when tracking agency publications during the transition. For sponsors and compliance teams, use the FDA's current official submission instructions and monitor any later substantive guidance revisions. The agency has not announced a blanket requirement to amend existing filings, labels or contracts because of this naming change.
