Bank Third-Party Risk Guidance: New December 16 Deadline

Federal banking agencies have moved the public-comment deadline for their proposed third-party risk management guidance to December 16. The extension gives financial institutions and other stakeholders more time to examine a proposal that is not yet binding.

By NewsTrendus Editorial Desk · November 16, 2026 · Source-linked desk reporting · Representative photograph

Representative licensed photograph of generic paperwork, not the proposed federal guidance. Photo: Leeloo The First / Pexels.

Quick answer: The Federal Reserve, FDIC, OCC and NCUA announced on November 13 that comments on their proposed third-party risk management guidance are now due December 16, 2026, instead of November 16. The agencies describe the proposed guidance as principles-based and nonbinding; it has not taken effect as final guidance.

What changed in the bank third-party risk guidance deadline?

The Federal Reserve's joint announcement confirms a one-month extension for public comments on the interagency proposal. The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency's separate publication corroborates the date and identifies the four participating agencies: the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, Federal Reserve Board, National Credit Union Administration and OCC.

The extension is a deadline for submitting feedback, not a compliance deadline for banks to implement a new rule. No final effective date was announced in these November 13 releases.

Who should pay attention to the proposal?

Banks, credit unions, third-party service providers and organizations working with financial institutions may find the proposal relevant. The agencies say their approach is intended to help supervised institutions tailor risk management to the risks posed by individual third-party relationships. That can include considering the nature of a service, the institution's reliance on it and the consequences if a provider fails, although the final supervisory expectations remain subject to the rulemaking process.

The announcement does not say that every vendor relationship must be handled identically. The agencies emphasize a principles-based approach rather than a uniform checklist. Readers should distinguish this proposed supervisory guidance from separate contractual obligations or existing applicable law.

What happens to existing agency guidance?

According to the joint release, when the agencies finalize the new guidance, they plan to rescind existing third-party risk management guidance and replace it with a common framework. The NCUA also expects to review its related guidance and make appropriate updates. Those changes are planned, not completed.

The Federal Reserve separately extended the comment deadline to December 16 for a proposed third-party risk management guide specifically aimed at Federal Reserve-supervised traditional community banks. That is a distinct proposal, and readers should consult the appropriate official notice before preparing a submission.

How can interested organizations respond before December 16?

Start with the Federal Reserve release and its linked Federal Register notices, which identify the proposal and the official comment instructions. Review the relevant notice, verify the docket and submission method, and send comments through the government channel specified there before the extended deadline. Do not assume that emailing a news outlet or a general agency contact address constitutes an official comment.

Useful feedback may explain operational implications for smaller institutions, how a proposed expectation could affect vendor oversight, or where language needs clarification. These are examples of possible stakeholder concerns, not claims about specific provisions of the draft.

What remains uncertain?

The agencies have not announced a final adoption date in the November 13 extension notices. The final text may change after comments are considered. Institutions should continue to follow existing applicable requirements and monitor the agencies' official announcements rather than treating the draft as already enacted.
