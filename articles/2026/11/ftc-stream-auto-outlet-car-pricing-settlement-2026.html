FTC Stream Auto Settlement: What Car Buyers Should Know

A proposed federal settlement with three Long Island dealerships challenges advertised vehicle prices that allegedly omitted required down payments, fees and add-ons. The order still requires a judge's approval.

Quick answer: On November 9, 2026, the Federal Trade Commission announced a proposed settlement with Stream Auto Outlet, alleging its Long Island dealerships advertised cars at prices that did not reflect required payments and charges. The proposed order would require prominent disclosure of the price buyers actually must pay, apart from government-required charges. A federal judge must approve the order before it becomes binding.

What did the FTC allege against Stream Auto Outlet?

The agency says Stream Auto Outlet's online vehicle advertisements drew shoppers with low headline prices, while required down payments of thousands of dollars appeared only in less prominent disclosures, including a details page behind a popup. According to the complaint, shoppers also encountered mandatory dealer fees and unwanted add-on charges. These are allegations in a government enforcement action, not findings reached after a trial.

The FTC says the group continued the alleged practices after receiving a March 2026 warning about advertising prices that omit required fees. The agency authorized the complaint and proposed stipulated order by a 2–0 vote, and the documents were filed in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of New York. Readers can examine the official complaint and proposed order rather than relying on a dealer's advertisement alone.

What would the proposed settlement require?

The proposed order would bar the named dealerships from misrepresenting the price or terms of acquiring a vehicle. Where an advertisement states a vehicle price, it would require the actual amount a consumer must pay to be displayed prominently, with only government-required charges excluded. The FTC emphasizes that the settlement is proposed: stipulated orders acquire the force of law once a federal judge approves and signs them.

This is not a nationwide court order directed at every dealership, nor does the FTC's announcement establish a refund program for Stream Auto customers. It illustrates the agency's existing enforcement approach to misleading advertising under the FTC Act.

Which car fees belong in the advertised price?

The FTC's September 2026 dealer-pricing guidance says a prominently advertised vehicle price should reflect the amount any consumer can pay, including mandatory dealer-imposed fees. Charges that a government requires the buyer to pay, such as applicable taxes or registration charges, are treated differently. Dealer document or processing fees are not automatically government charges merely because state law regulates them.

A discount restricted to a particular buyer group or financing arrangement should not be presented as the universally available price. Negotiation may reduce a final price, but the starting advertisement should not depend on an undisclosed rebate, required add-on or payment condition. The guidance explains FTC staff's interpretation; it is not itself a new nationwide regulation.

How can buyers check an out-the-door car price?

Before visiting a dealership, save the vehicle listing and ask for a written, itemized out-the-door price tied to the vehicle identification number. Request separate lines for the vehicle, dealer-required charges, government taxes and registration, and optional products. Ask whether the advertised price assumes dealer financing, a trade-in or a rebate for which not every shopper qualifies.

Before signing, compare the written offer with the buyer's order and financing documents. If a mandatory charge appears that was not reflected in the ad, request a clear explanation and preserve the paperwork. Consumers who believe they encountered deceptive advertising can submit details to the FTC through ReportFraud.ftc.gov. Reporting a concern does not guarantee compensation or determine whether a specific dealership violated the law.

What happens next in the Stream Auto case?

The next legal milestone is judicial review of the proposed stipulated order. The FTC's November 9 announcement does not provide a date for a ruling or say that a judge has already entered a final order. The case documents are the best primary record for any subsequent change in status.
