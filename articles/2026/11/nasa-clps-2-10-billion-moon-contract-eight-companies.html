NASA's $10 Billion Moon Contract: Who Can Compete?

NASA has opened a $10 billion ceiling for future lunar cargo delivery competitions. The eight selected firms have not each won a $10 billion award.

By NewsTrendus Editorial Desk · November 9, 2026 · NASA announcement dated November 7

Quick answer: On November 7, 2026, NASA named eight U.S. space companies eligible to compete for Moon deliveries under CLPS 2. The program has a $10 billion maximum contract ceiling over 10 years; that is not a $10 billion payment, spending commitment, or award to each company. NASA separately announced a $94 million base task order for Blue Origin to deliver the PROMISE lunar rover, with an option potentially worth $140 million more. Read NASA's November 7 release.

Which eight companies are eligible for NASA's CLPS 2 contract?

The selected providers are Blue Origin, Firefly Aerospace, Intuitive Machines, ispace-U.S., Lockheed Martin Space, Northrop Grumman Space Systems, SpaceX, and Voyager Technologies. They can compete for later task orders to transport NASA scientific instruments, technology demonstrations and Moon Base infrastructure to the lunar surface. Eligibility does not guarantee any company a particular delivery, revenue amount or launch date.

Does the $10 billion figure mean NASA already spent $10 billion?

No. CLPS 2 is an indefinite-delivery, indefinite-quantity multiple-award arrangement. The $10 billion is the combined maximum value of orders that may be placed across the selected providers during the contract period. Individual deliveries must still be ordered and awarded. That distinction matters for taxpayers, space-sector investors and suppliers evaluating headlines that describe the program as a single giant contract.

The values refer to different procurement mechanisms. They should not be added together as if they were all immediately spent or paid out. NASA's CLPS overview explains the broader approach of purchasing delivery services from commercial providers rather than owning every lander.

What is the PROMISE rover and when might it launch?

PROMISE stands for Polar Rover for Observation, Mapping, and In-Situ Exploration. NASA says the six-wheeled, nuclear-powered vehicle will study the Moon's South Pole, including possible subsurface resources, radiation and temperature conditions. Its drill is designed to investigate material roughly one meter below the surface. The agency is reusing spare and test hardware derived from its Mars rover programs, rather than describing PROMISE as a completely new rover built from scratch.

NASA currently targets a late-2028 launch and an 18-month primary mission. Those are targets, not guaranteed arrival dates. The PROMISE mission page provides the official mission description and will be the better source for subsequent schedule changes.

What other Moon Base projects did NASA announce?

NASA also announced six transportation task orders involving Arrow Science and Technology, Blue Origin and Impulse Space, a 2028 cislunar communications demonstration assigned to SpaceX, and work on a small combined solar-power and communications tower. Those projects address different challenges: moving cargo between orbits, maintaining links with lunar equipment, and supplying power near the lunar surface.

These are separate from a crewed lunar landing award. The November 7 announcement does not mean an astronaut base is already constructed or that all named technologies have completed flight testing.

What happens next for U.S. space companies and readers?

The practical next milestones are the award of specific CLPS 2 delivery orders, testing of transportation and communications systems, and PROMISE integration ahead of its targeted 2028 launch. Watch NASA's original release for contract details rather than treating the ceiling as booked company revenue.
