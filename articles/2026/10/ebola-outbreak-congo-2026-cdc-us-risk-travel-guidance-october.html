Ebola Outbreak 2026: CDC Says U.S. Risk Is Low as Congo Cases Top 10,000

The outbreak is severe in Central Africa, but the CDC says no related cases have been confirmed in the United States. Travelers face specific restrictions.

By NewsTrendus Editorial Desk · October 27, 2026 · Independently written from credited sources · Representative editorial photograph

Representative licensed photograph, not the reported event or people. Photo: Mikhail Nilov / Pexels (Pexels free-use license).

At a glance: CDC's October 27, 2026 Ebola update reports more than 10,000 Congo cases, no linked U.S. cases and low domestic risk. Latest counts and travel guidance.

ATLANTA, Oct. 27, 2026 — The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention says the 2026 Bundibugyo Ebola outbreak has reached 10,060 confirmed cases in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, with 4,887 confirmed deaths there, based on data through October 25. The CDC's October 27 update reports no confirmed U.S. cases linked to this outbreak and describes the overall risk to the American public as low.

What are the latest confirmed Ebola numbers?

The CDC's October 27 situation page lists 10,091 confirmed cases and 4,891 confirmed deaths across affected countries as of October 25. The Democratic Republic of the Congo accounts for 10,060 of those confirmed cases and 4,887 deaths. The page also lists nine confirmed cases and two deaths in Kenya, one confirmed case in Nigeria, 20 confirmed cases in Uganda and one in France, while separately noting probable cases in Uganda.

These figures are a dated snapshot and may change as health authorities reconcile reports. They should not be treated as live counts. The CDC calls this the second-largest Ebola outbreak on record, and the World Health Organization has described substantial challenges from insecurity, access to care and cross-border movement.

Is Ebola spreading in the United States?

No U.S. case associated with the current outbreak had been confirmed as of the CDC's October 27 update. The agency considers the likelihood of importation very low and says the risk of onward transmission would also be low if a case were diagnosed, citing public-health and infection-control capacity.

That assessment does not diminish the emergency for people in affected African communities. Risk varies dramatically by exposure, geography and healthcare conditions. A rising case count overseas should not be presented as evidence of an American outbreak when U.S. surveillance reports no linked cases.

What is Bundibugyo Ebola and how does it spread?

The outbreak involves Bundibugyo virus, one of the viruses that cause Ebola disease. Transmission requires contact with infectious body fluids or contaminated materials; it is not the same as routine exposure to someone simply passing by in a public place. Symptoms can include fever, fatigue, headache, vomiting and diarrhea, but these symptoms alone do not diagnose Ebola.

The WHO says there is no approved vaccine or specific treatment for the Bundibugyo species at present, although research is underway. Medical evaluation and supportive care remain critical. People with a relevant travel or exposure history and symptoms should contact healthcare or public-health authorities for instructions before visiting a facility.

What does the CDC advise U.S. travelers?

The CDC's October 27 guidance includes temporary entry restrictions involving recent travel to the Democratic Republic of the Congo and designated screening rules for certain other travel histories. It advises avoiding all travel to Ituri and North Kivu and avoiding nonessential travel to Haut-Uele and Tshopo. Because requirements can change, travelers should read the official CDC page immediately before making plans.

The agency also describes a November 3 effective date for an additional airline attestation requirement related to travel in the DRC. That is a prospective measure as of this report, not a requirement already in effect on October 27. Individual circumstances and transit routes matter.

What should readers take away?

Two statements can both be true: the Congo outbreak is a major international health emergency, and the CDC currently assesses risk to the general U.S. public as low. Accurate coverage should state both, give the date of the case counts and direct readers to official travel and clinical guidance rather than spread alarming rumors.

This report is public-health information, not a diagnosis. If someone may have had a high-risk exposure, they should follow local health department instructions and current CDC recommendations.
