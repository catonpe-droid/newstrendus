Fed Holds Rates at 3.75%–4% on October 28: Two Officials Wanted a Hike

The Federal Reserve paused after September's increase, but two policymakers favored another quarter-point hike as inflation stayed elevated.

By NewsTrendus Editorial Desk · October 28, 2026 · Independently written from credited sources · Representative editorial photograph

At a glance: The Fed held its benchmark rate at 3.75%–4% on October 28, 2026. See the 10–2 vote, dissenters and what the decision means for U.S. borrowers.

WASHINGTON, Oct. 28, 2026 — The Federal Open Market Committee kept its target federal funds rate at 3.75% to 4.00% on Wednesday. The decision passed by a 10–2 vote, according to the Federal Reserve's statement released at 2 p.m. Eastern. It was a hold, not a rate cut, and it followed September's quarter-point increase.

What did the Fed decide at the October 2026 meeting?

The official statement kept the benchmark policy range unchanged and said the central bank would continue maintaining ample reserves in the banking system. The federal funds rate is the overnight rate targeted for transactions between banks; it is not the interest rate that every U.S. consumer pays on a loan.

The committee described economic activity as expanding at a strong pace, with resilient domestic spending, robust capital investment and little change in unemployment. At the same time, it said inflation remained elevated and uncertainty was high, partly because of geopolitical developments. Those conditions help explain why policymakers chose not to loosen monetary policy.

Who voted against holding interest rates?

Federal Reserve Governor Lisa D. Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth M. Hammack voted against the action. Both preferred to raise the target range by 0.25 percentage point. The 10–2 result is important: the disagreement was over whether to tighten policy further, not whether to cut rates. The Federal Reserve explicitly identified both dissenters in its October 28 release.

That split gives investors and households a useful signal about the inflation debate, but it does not predict the result of the next meeting. Members can change their views when employment, prices and other economic indicators change.

What does the decision mean for mortgage, savings and credit-card rates?

A Fed hold leaves the central bank's short-term benchmark unchanged today. It does not freeze mortgage rates, which respond to longer-term Treasury yields, lender pricing, credit risk and market expectations. Existing fixed-rate mortgage borrowers generally will not see their contractual rate change because of this vote alone.

Variable-rate products can react differently. Credit-card APRs and some home-equity lines are linked to prime rates, which often track Fed moves; with no policy change, borrowers should not expect an automatic quarter-point reduction. Savings-account and certificate-of-deposit rates are set by individual institutions and can change even without an FOMC move.

For people comparing loans, the practical calculation remains the same: check the quoted annual percentage rate, fees, term and monthly payment rather than treating the headline federal funds rate as a consumer borrowing quote.

Why is inflation still the central question?

The Fed reiterated its 2% inflation objective and said additional information would help it judge the appropriate policy stance. A strong economy and stubborn price pressures can give officials reasons to keep rates restrictive, while signs of weakening hiring or demand could change the balance. Wednesday's statement did not commit to a December increase or promise a future cut.

Independent reporting by Axios also noted the two dissents and the committee's decision to stand pat. The most authoritative record of the vote, however, is the Federal Reserve's own statement.

What should Americans watch next?

The next useful inputs are upcoming inflation readings, labor-market reports and public remarks from policymakers. Anyone considering a home purchase, refinance or high-interest debt payoff should compare live lender offers rather than assuming a rate move will arrive on a fixed date. The October 28 decision establishes the current policy range, not a guaranteed path for loan rates or financial markets.
