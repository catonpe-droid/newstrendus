New Mauritian Tidal Trapdoor Spider: How a Species Survives Ocean Tides

A spider first spotted on a tiny Mauritius island in 2023 has now been formally described, revealing an unusual life in wave-washed coastal rock.

By NewsTrendus Editorial Desk · October 28, 2026 · Independently written from credited sources · Representative editorial photograph

Representative licensed photograph, not the reported event, product or person. Photo: Rafael Minguet Delgado / Pexels (Pexels free-use license).

At a glance: Scientists formally described Idioctis marevallum, a trapdoor spider living in coastal rock in Mauritius. Its discovery, habitat and conservation questions.

Scientists working with the Durrell Wildlife Conservation Trust have formally described a new spider species, Idioctis marevallum, after its discovery during conservation surveys on a tiny island off Mauritius. The Mauritian tidal trapdoor spider lives in rock cavities regularly reached by seawater — an unusual setting for an animal related to trapdoor spiders and tarantulas.

Where was the Mauritian tidal trapdoor spider discovered?

Researchers first noticed the animal in 2023 on Ilot Vacoas, an island of roughly one hectare off the southeast coast of Mauritius. The finding emerged from the Mauritius Island Restoration Programme, which involves Durrell Wildlife Conservation Trust and local conservation partners. A formal scientific description was publicized in October 2026, which is why the species is now appearing in news and search trends.

According to the conservation group and independent coverage by ITV News, the spider has also been found on Ile aux Fouquets, another small island in the same region. These observations are not evidence that it is widespread across Mauritius or other parts of the Indian Ocean.

How can a trapdoor spider live where tides reach its home?

The species occupies the intertidal zone, the stretch of shore that is alternately exposed and submerged or splashed by waves. Instead of digging a conventional burrow in soil, it uses narrow tubes in coralline rock. Researchers believe the cavities were originally made by marine organisms such as molluscs and worms long ago.

It closes the entrance with a camouflaged trapdoor made from silk, sand and algae. The barrier blends with the surrounding surface and helps protect the spider in a habitat where ordinary burrow-dwelling species might struggle. Exactly how it tolerates saltwater exposure, and for how long, remains a question for further research; a striking observation should not be stretched into an unsupported claim about its physiology.

Why did scientists overlook the species for so long?

The team was surveying coastal habitats during island restoration work, not searching for a high-profile new spider. One researcher noticed legs projecting from a small hole during a nighttime survey. The tiny habitat, the animal's camouflage and the fact that its shelter resembles a natural rock opening all make casual detection difficult.

Finding a species that has not been formally described is different from claiming the animal only recently evolved. The spider may have occupied this habitat for a long time; what changed was scientific documentation and classification.

Is this spider dangerous to people?

Neither the conservation announcement nor the cited reporting establishes a specific medical risk to humans from Idioctis marevallum. The species belongs to a group that includes trapdoor spiders, but membership in that group alone does not justify claims that it is deadly, aggressive or harmless. People should avoid disturbing unfamiliar wildlife and allow specialists to study the animals in their habitat.

What does the discovery mean for conservation?

Islands can support highly localized species that are easy to miss in broader biodiversity surveys. The research team also documented spider species not previously recorded in Mauritius, highlighting how incomplete species inventories can be even in well-visited regions.

Because the new spider is known from limited island locations, monitoring its habitat could be important. But no reliable population estimate or formal conservation-threat category was provided in the cited announcement. Claims that the spider is already endangered would therefore go beyond the available evidence.

For U.S. readers following science news, the larger lesson is that biodiversity discoveries are not limited to remote forests or deep oceans: even a small wave-washed shoreline can contain species that science has not yet named.
