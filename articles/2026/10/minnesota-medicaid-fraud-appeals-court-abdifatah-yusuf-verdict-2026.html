Minnesota Appeals Court Restores Guilty Verdicts in $7.2 Million Medicaid Fraud Case

A three-judge panel ordered reinstatement of six jury verdicts. The ruling concerns the sufficiency of trial evidence, not a newly filed criminal charge.

By NewsTrendus Editorial Desk · October 27, 2026 · Independently written from credited sources · Representative editorial photograph

Representative licensed photograph, not the reported event or people. Photo: Taha Samet Arslan / Pexels (Pexels free-use license).

At a glance: On October 26, 2026, the Minnesota Court of Appeals reversed a district court's post-verdict acquittal of Abdifatah Abdulkadir Yusuf and remanded for reinstatement of six jury guilty verdicts in a Medicaid fraud case involving more than $7.2 million.

What did the Minnesota Court of Appeals decide?

ST. PAUL, Minn., Oct. 27, 2026 — The Minnesota Court of Appeals has reversed a district court's post-verdict acquittal of Abdifatah Abdulkadir Yusuf in a Medicaid fraud case and sent the matter back for reinstatement of six jury guilty verdicts. The decision was filed October 26, 2026, in State of Minnesota v. Yusuf, appellate case A25-2003.

The 18-page appellate opinion says the evidence was sufficient under the relevant circumstantial-evidence test. It is a reversal and remand, not a report that a new trial has already occurred or that a final sentence was issued October 26.

How did the case reach the appeals court?

Yusuf was charged in 2024 over Medicaid reimbursements associated with Promise Health Services, a provider of home- and community-based services. In August 2025, a Hennepin County jury returned guilty verdicts on six counts of aiding and abetting theft by swindle. After the trial, District Judge Sarah West granted a defense motion for judgment of acquittal, reasoning that the proof did not exclude a reasonable explanation that Yusuf's brother carried out the fraud without his knowledge.

Minnesota Attorney General Keith Ellison's office appealed. The three-judge appellate panel, with an opinion by Judge Renee Worke, disagreed with the lower court's assessment of the record and ordered reinstatement of the jury verdicts. The Attorney General's October 26 statement describes the prosecution's position and the procedural result.

What evidence did the appellate panel emphasize?

The opinion describes Promise's billing for services that were not delivered or lacked required documentation, as well as its use of a mailbox rather than a legitimate operating office. It notes Yusuf's ownership and managerial filings, his role in payroll and payments, and financial evidence showing more than $1 million flowing to him personally from Promise accounts.

According to the court's analysis, the combined circumstances supported the jury's finding that Yusuf knew fraudulent billing was occurring and intentionally aided his brother. The opinion is more precise than an accusation based on a single purchase or a political talking point: the panel considered the evidentiary record as a whole and explained its legal standard.

Why does the $7.2 million figure matter?

The court describes more than 40,000 reimbursement claims and approximately $7.2 million in fraud attributed to Promise in the trial evidence. This is the amount associated with the provider scheme, not a claim that Yusuf personally received $7.2 million. The distinction matters when readers encounter headlines conflating the total fraud amount with individual account transfers.

These programs are designed to help eligible people receive assistance with activities at home and in the community. Billing for services not provided can divert funds from the program and undermine confidence in legitimate providers. The decision also illustrates the legal difference between a jury's verdict and a judge's later review of evidentiary sufficiency.

What happens next, and is the decision precedential?

The case returns to district court after the appellate reversal. The opinion directs reinstatement of the six jury verdicts; it does not itself announce a new sentencing date or sentence. The first page expressly labels the opinion nonprecedential except as allowed under Minnesota's appellate rules. Further proceedings, if any, should be checked through official court records.

The Minnesota Star Tribune independently reported the appellate decision. This article is based on the published court document and the state announcement, not on social-media commentary about the judge or defendant.
